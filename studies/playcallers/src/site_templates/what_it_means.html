What it means

Twenty-seven seasons of play callers, followed from sideline to sideline, and an honest account of how much a number can say about the man holding the laminated card.

The question

A coordinator leaves and the offense is never quite the same. A defensive coach takes a head job and within a year his new team is playing the defense his old team played. Fans notice these things, and they are usually right to. Ben Johnson ran Detroit's offense for three seasons and then went to Chicago. Mike Macdonald ran Baltimore's defense and then went to Seattle. The idea behind this study is simple: if play calling is a skill a man owns, it should travel with him, and a career of results should add up to a record.

The quarterback in the room

The hardest result in the study is this one. Take every offense and try to predict how good it was from who called the plays and who played quarterback. A model that knows only the quarterback does about as well as one that knows both ({{ facts.qb_model_qb_only_mse|f3 }} against {{ facts.qb_model_crossed_mse|f3 }}). A model that knows only the play caller does clearly worse ({{ facts.qb_model_caller_only_mse|f3 }}). Most of what an offensive play caller's record measures is the quarterback he had.

That does not make the play caller irrelevant. It means scores cannot easily tell the two apart, because great quarterbacks and respected play callers tend to stay together. The quarterback-adjusted list rewards callers who produced with several different quarterbacks, and it moves Mike Shanahan and Greg Roman up and quarterbacks' long-time partners down. It is the fairer list and the less certain one.

What happens when they move

When a new offensive play caller arrives, the offense improves by {{ ms[('offense','arrival_change')].mean|f2 }} on average. That sounds like proof until it is set beside what bad offenses do anyway: those in the bottom third of the league improve by {{ ms[('offense','baseline_tercile_low')].mean|f2 }} the following season, new caller or not. Teams change play callers when things have gone badly, and things that have gone badly tend to get better.

The same holds in reverse. Offenses decline by {{ (-ms[('offense','departure_change')].mean)|f2 }} after losing a caller, and offenses in the top third decline by {{ (-ms[('offense','baseline_tercile_high')].mean)|f2 }} on their own. Detroit's offense after Johnson fits the pattern, and so would most very good offenses the year after a career season. The individual cases on the movers page are more interesting than the averages, and they are there to be read one at a time.

Where the numbers stop

Three limits are worth carrying. First, who called the plays is a guess for a large share of seasons, and the older the season the shakier the guess; every table says how much of a career rests on guesses. Second, a play caller's record includes everything about his team that he did not control: the quarterback, the line, injuries, and on defense the offense that kept handing him short fields. Third, {{ facts.n_excluded|n }} offenses and defenses are left out because the caller changed during the season or could not be identified, and some of those seasons would change a career.

What survives those limits is modest and, we think, real: records that match what people who watched remember, two young coaches whose results have travelled, and a clear warning about how much credit the man with the card should get when the man under center is one of the best who ever played.
