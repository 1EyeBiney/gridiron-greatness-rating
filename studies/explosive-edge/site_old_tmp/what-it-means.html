What It Means

Twenty-seven seasons, 1.3 million plays, and a theory from the broadcast booth that turns out to be wrong about Sunday and right about the season.

The theory

For as long as anyone has kept the stat, the turnover battle has been the first thing a coach mentions at the podium and the first number an analyst reaches for. Give the ball away and you hand the other side a possession; do it twice and you have probably lost. Chris Simms' argument is that the modern game has added a second column to that ledger. Offenses, he says, have learned that a twelve-play drive is twelve chances for the defense to make a play - a sack, a strip, a tipped ball - and so they hunt the explosive play instead: get the chunk, shorten the drive, get off the field with points before the defense can get its hands on the ball. If that is how the game is now played, then the team with more big plays should be winning, more and more, and the turnover battle should be losing some of its grip.

This study takes that seriously enough to test each piece.

Sunday still belongs to the turnover

Take every regular-season game since 1999 and ask which single number better tells you who won: the difference in explosive plays, or the difference in turnovers. It isn't close, and it hasn't gotten close. Knowing only the turnover battle picks the winner 72.5% of the time over the last nine seasons. Knowing only the explosive battle picks it 66.4% of the time. Put in points, a turnover has been worth about 4.1 points of final margin and an explosive play about 1.8, which makes one turnover worth a little over two big plays - and that exchange rate is the same now as it was when Kurt Warner was throwing to Isaac Bruce.

The bar-argument version is blunter. Your team wins the big-play battle and loses the turnover battle. How often does it win the game? 34.6% of the time in the first nine seasons of the data, 38.2% in the last nine. Better, a little. Still a loss more often than not. If the argument is about who wins this game, the turnover side of the table is still right.

The mechanism isn't there either

The shorter-drive story is the part of the theory that can be checked most directly, and it fails. Drives are longer than they were, not shorter: 5.3 plays and about 2.4 minutes of clock per possession in 1999, 6.0 plays and 2.9 minutes in 2025. The rate at which a defense makes a play - a sack, a takeaway, a tackle behind the line - has fallen from 11.3% of snaps to 9.2%. There is no rising per-snap danger for an offense to hurry away from; the modern defense gets its hands on fewer plays, not more. And the teams that generate the most explosive plays don't run shorter drives than everyone else. They run slightly longer ones. Offenses aren't gambling on the big play to escape the defense. They are moving the ball more efficiently across the board, and the big plays come with it.

What has changed dramatically is the thing the theory was supposed to displace. Turnovers have fallen from 3.8 a game to 2.3 - a drop of about forty percent. Quarterbacks throw fewer interceptions, ball carriers lose fewer fumbles, and the rules protect the passer. A turnover is worth as much as it ever was. There are simply fewer of them, which means the turnover battle is a draw more often, and in a draw the big plays decide.

Where Simms is right, for a reason he didn't give

Here is the result that changes the argument. Take every team-season and split it in half. How well does the first half predict the second? Margin of victory - the most information-rich number there is - carries over with a correlation of about 0.49. Explosive-play differential carries over at 0.42. Turnover differential carries over at 0.18, barely above nothing, in every era.

Read that as a statement about what kind of thing each number is. A team that is winning the turnover battle in October has, mostly, been lucky, and its luck will not follow it into December. A team that is winning the big-play battle in October is doing something - protecting the quarterback, hitting deep, tackling in space, making the other side drive the length of the field - that it will still be doing in December. Turnovers decide games and then evaporate. Big plays decide games less often and persist. If you want to know who won on Sunday, count the turnovers. If you want to know who is good, count the big plays.

That shows up when you put this study next to the main site's True Strength Rating, which rates every team-season by margin and schedule. Explosive-play differential correlates with a team's rating at about 0.65, and turnover differential at about 0.65 - the same - but one of those is a trait and the other is largely a record of good fortune. The most explosive team-seasons since 1999 read like a list of teams you remember: the 2014 SEA (12-4), the 2012 SF (11-4-1), the 2023 SF (12-5), the 2019 BAL (14-2), the 2001 PIT (13-3). Three of the five reached a Super Bowl.

One honest caveat

Part of the persistence gap is arithmetic. A team produces about nine explosive plays a game and gives the ball away barely more than once, and a rare event is measured with more noise than a common one, so some of "turnovers don't repeat" is really "turnovers are rare." Not all of it - the gap is too wide, and it holds year to year as well as half to half - but the site says so where it reports the numbers rather than letting the cleaner story stand alone. There is a second caveat too, stated on the methodology page: explosive plays are yards, yards are points, and so "big plays predict wins" is partly a tautology. Every comparison here is therefore relative - against turnovers, and across time - and the result holds under a definition of explosive that uses expected points instead of yards.

What to take from it

The broadcast version of the theory - that big plays have caught turnovers as the thing that decides a game - is not supported, and the story about shorter drives and defenses waiting to pounce is backwards. But the instinct behind it is sound. The game has fewer turnovers than it used to, so the turnover battle settles fewer games. And the explosive play is the better measure of what a team actually is, because it is the one that shows up again next week. Coaches who build for it aren't chasing a fad. They are chasing the part of winning that they can keep.
