Do big plays decide games now?

6,964 regular-season games, 1999 to 2025, every play of them. The claim under test: explosive plays now belong next to turnovers as the thing that decides who wins.

The short version

  • Turnovers still decide games. In every era since 1999, the turnover differential predicts the winner better than the explosive-play differential does. Knowing only the turnover battle gets you 72.5% of winners right in 2017-2025; knowing only the explosive battle gets you 66.4%. A turnover is worth about 4.1 points of margin and an explosive play about 1.8, so one turnover equals roughly 2.3 big plays. That exchange rate has barely moved in 27 years.
  • Win the big-play battle and lose the turnover battle, and you usually lose. 34.6% of such games in 1999-2007, 38.2% in 2017-2025. A little better than it was. Still a losing proposition.
  • But big plays decide seasons. A team's explosive-play differential over the first half of a season predicts its second half with a correlation of 0.42 (2017-2025). Its turnover differential: 0.18. Big plays are a repeatable trait. Turnovers are mostly weather. And explosive differential tracks the main site's True Strength Rating as closely as turnover differential does.
  • The mechanism Simms describes isn't there. Drives have gotten longer, not shorter: 5.3 plays and 146 seconds per drive in 1999, 6.0 plays and 172 seconds in 2025. Defenses make a play (sack, takeaway, or tackle for loss) on 9.2% of snaps now, down from 11.3%. There is no rising per-snap danger for offenses to hurry away from.
  • What did change is that turnovers are vanishing. 3.8 per game in 1999, 2.3 in 2025. The turnover battle is a draw more often than it used to be, and when it is, the explosive battle decides.

Split every team's season in half and ask how well the first half predicts the second. Margin of victory, the benchmark, holds up at about 0.49. Explosive-play differential holds up at 0.42. Turnover differential barely holds up at all: 0.18. That gap is the strongest thing in this study, and it points the way Simms is pointing, for a reason he doesn't quite give: not because big plays swing games more than turnovers do (they don't), but because a team that makes them will keep making them, while a team that is winning the turnover battle in October is mostly getting lucky. Part of that gap is arithmetic - a team has about nine explosive plays a game and barely one turnover, so the rarer event is measured with more noise - and the skill-or-luck page says so. The gap survives the caveat.

Your team out-gained theirs on big plays but threw two picks. Who won? Usually them: 37.9% for the big-play team over the whole period. Win both battles and you win 90.0% of the time. Win only the explosive battle with turnovers even: 70.7%. Win only the turnover battle with explosives even: 82.1%. Strip out garbage time and the big-play team does a few points better, but it still loses more than it wins. The head-to-head page has it by season.
