Methodology

Definitions, data source, and the modelling choices behind every table on this site. This page is factual only - it does not draw conclusions about the results.

The question

Turnover differential is the classic single-number explanation for who won an NFL game. The claim tested here is that explosive plays now belong alongside it: that the team with more big plays wins, that this reflects a real shift in how offenses attack, and that the shift has grown over time. That is broken into four separate, testable pieces: association (does explosive-play differential predict the winner as well as turnover differential, and has that changed), exchange rate (how many points is each worth, and has the rate moved), mechanism (are drives getting shorter and more explosive, and is that a deliberate trade-off), and persistence (is explosive-play differential more repeatable than turnover differential, i.e. closer to a skill).

A caution stated here and in the findings: explosive plays gain yards, and yards become points, so "more big plays predicts more wins" is close to a tautology on its own. The informative comparisons are relative (explosive plays versus turnovers) and over time (has the balance shifted), not the absolute size of any one coefficient.

Data source and license

Play-by-play data comes from nflverse (nflfastR), 1999-2025, distributed as one Parquet file per season through the nflverse-data GitHub releases, under a CC BY 4.0 license. Raw play-by-play is not committed to this repository. An ingest script downloads each season's file to a local, gitignored directory, aggregates it to one row per team-game, and only that aggregate (data/processed/team_game.csv, roughly 14,000 rows) is committed. The site and every test in this repository are built from that aggregate and the analysis tables derived from it, so no part of the build or test suite re-downloads play-by-play data. The main project's game table supplies the True Strength Rating (TSR) used on the leaderboard page, joined on season and franchise.

Definitions

Every count below is computed per team-game, on scrimmage plays: play_type is pass or run, the nflverse play flag is set (excluding penalty-only or no-play rows), and the play is not a quarterback kneel or spike.

  • Explosive play, primary definition: a pass gaining 20 or more yards, or a rush gaining 10 or more. Three sensitivity variants are reported alongside it throughout: 25 yards passing / 15 rushing; 20/20; and an EPA-based definition (any scrimmage play with EPA >= +2.0). EPA is expected points added: how much the play moved the offense's expected score on that drive. It is not win probability added (WPA). The EPA variant removes the fixed yardage threshold and rewards a play for what it was worth in the situation, but expected points are still built from field position and yards, so it softens the yardage tautology rather than removing it.
  • Turnover: an interception or a lost fumble, on any play type including special teams (a turnover on a punt still hands over a possession). A scrimmage-only variant is tracked too. Turnovers are attributed to whichever team actually had the ball when it came loose, not always the team with possession on the play (see the module docstring in xe_metrics.py for the punt-return edge case this matters for).
  • Defensive "play": a play credits a team's defense once if it produced at least one of a sack, a forced turnover, or a tackle for loss (so a strip-sack counts once, not twice). The per-snap rate of this is the mechanism metric for whether offenses face more per-play risk than they used to.
  • Drive metrics: plays per drive and seconds per drive, from the play-by-play drive fields.
  • Differentials (suffixed _diff) are always team minus opponent within the same game.
  • Garbage time: every explosive-play and turnover count has a non-garbage-time twin (suffixed _ng), restricted to plays where the offense's win probability was between 5% and 95%. Every headline analysis is run on all plays; the _ng variant is reported alongside as a sensitivity check, not as the default.

Modelling

The association analysis (win ~ explosive differential + turnover differential) and the exchange-rate analysis (margin ~ explosive differential + turnover differential) are both fit on one row per game: the home team's row from the team-game table, for regular-season games only, 1999-2025. Differentials in that row are already home-minus-away, so a regression on the home rows alone uses every game exactly once without double-counting it from both teams' perspectives.

Association is a logistic regression of the game winner on both differentials, standardized to z-scores within each season so seasons with different raw scales are comparable; ties are dropped for this model, since a binary win/loss is undefined for them, and the standardization is computed after dropping ties so the scale matches the fit sample. Three versions are fit every time: explosive differential alone, turnover differential alone, and both together, each reporting a coefficient, its standard error, log loss, and classification accuracy. The exchange-rate analysis is an ordinary least-squares regression of the same idea on raw (unstandardized) units, so its coefficients are directly in points: points per explosive play, points per turnover, and their ratio (how many explosive plays one turnover is worth), with a delta-method standard error on the ratio. Margin is defined for ties, so ties are kept in this model. Both models are fit with a small custom implementation (NumPy IRLS for the logistic regression, closed-form least squares for the linear one) rather than a third-party statistics library.

Persistence is measured by splitting each team-season's games in half by week number (the earlier half getting the smaller share when the season length is odd) and correlating the first half's rate against the second half's, per team-season, within era. Year-to-year persistence instead correlates a team's full-season rate in one season against its rate the following season. Both use a Pearson correlation with a 95% confidence interval from the Fisher z-transform.

Eras

Three nine-season blocks spanning the full sample: 1999-2007, 2008-2016, and 2017-2025. They are used throughout instead of decades so each era is the same length and the most recent one is complete.

Quarterback measures

Three separate things are reported and never mixed. Passing explosiveness is a quarterback's own completions of 20 or more yards divided by his own dropbacks (pass attempts, sacks taken, and scrambles), credited play by play from the play-by-play file's passer and rusher fields, regular season only; a companion column gives the share of those completions that travelled 20 or more yards in the air. Quarterback rushing explosiveness is his scrambles and designed runs gaining 10 or more yards per such rush. Team offensive explosiveness is every explosive play over every scrimmage play, on the leaderboard page. The continuity split uses the schedule file's starting quarterback per game, joined on franchise rather than team code (the play-by-play carries each franchise's current code for its whole history; the schedule file carries the code of the day). The first published version of the leaderboard credited each starter with his team's whole-game explosive total, runs included, and dropped 894 Rams, Chargers and Raiders team-games in that join; both were found by an outside reviewer and are corrected here.

Limitations

  • Because explosive plays mechanically produce yards and yards produce points, any measure of "does explosive differential predict wins" carries some of that tautology built in. The comparisons that matter are relative to turnover differential and over time, not the absolute size of a coefficient.
  • The game-level regressions explain games that have already been played: the explosive plays and turnovers in them are part of how the final score happened. They are descriptions, fitted and reported on the same sample, not forecasts. The one forward-looking test on the site is the regular-season-to-playoffs regression, which uses only information available before the postseason began.
  • "No detectable effect" is not "no effect." Where a coefficient is reported as indistinguishable from zero, the standard error beside it gives the range of effects the data still allow.
  • The quarterback-continuity split is an association. Keeping or changing a quarterback is not randomly assigned, and a team that keeps one usually keeps its scheme and receivers too. Separating the passer's share would need the four-way comparison (departing and receiving team, before and after each move) that this study has not built.
  • nflverse's play-by-play team codes reflect each franchise's current code for its entire history (for example, the 1999 Raiders appear as "LV"). The crosswalk used to normalize team codes to franchise-stable IDs handles this for codes it has seen elsewhere in the data; a handful of early-era rows can still carry an un-normalized code, which shows up as unmatched rows against the main project's team ratings (leaderboard_unmatched.csv on the data page).
  • The 2026 season, in progress at the time of writing, is excluded from every table and analysis on this site.

Reproducibility

Everything is scripted. The ingest script re-downloads and re-aggregates play-by-play data on request; every analysis table and every page on this site is regenerated from data/processed/team_game.csv and the tables derived from it. The repository has the code; the data page has every table as CSV.
