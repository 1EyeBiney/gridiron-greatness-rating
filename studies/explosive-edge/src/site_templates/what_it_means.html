What It Means

Twenty-seven seasons, 1.3 million plays, and a theory from the broadcast booth that turns out to be wrong about Sunday and right about the season.

The theory

For as long as anyone has kept the stat, the turnover battle has been the first thing a coach mentions at the podium and the first number an analyst reaches for. Give the ball away and you hand the other side a possession; do it twice and you have probably lost. Chris Simms' argument is that the modern game has added a second column to that ledger. Offenses, he says, have learned that a twelve-play drive is twelve chances for the defense to make a play - a sack, a strip, a tipped ball - and so they hunt the explosive play instead: get the chunk, shorten the drive, get off the field with points before the defense can get its hands on the ball. If that is how the game is now played, then the team with more big plays should be winning, more and more, and the turnover battle should be losing some of its grip.

This study takes that seriously enough to test each piece.

Sunday still belongs to the turnover

Take every regular-season game since 1999 and ask which single number better tells you who won: the difference in explosive plays, or the difference in turnovers. On the raw numbers it isn't close, and it hasn't gotten close. Knowing only the turnover battle picks the winner {{ s3.tod_only_accuracy|pct }} of the time over the last nine seasons. Knowing only the explosive battle picks it {{ s3.epd_only_accuracy|pct }} of the time. Put in points, a turnover has been worth about {{ (-x3.points_per_turnover)|f1 }} points of final margin and an explosive play about {{ x3.points_per_explosive|f1 }}, which makes one turnover worth a little over two big plays - and that exchange rate is the same now as it was when Kurt Warner was throwing to Isaac Bruce.

The bar-argument version is blunter. Your team wins the big-play battle and loses the turnover battle. How often does it win the game? {{ h1.won_explosive_lost_turnover.win_rate|pct }} of the time in the first nine seasons of the data, {{ h3.won_explosive_lost_turnover.win_rate|pct }} in the last nine. Better, a little. Still a loss more often than not. If the argument is about who wins this game, the turnover side of the table is still right.

It is right by less than it first appears, though. A good share of the turnover's predictive power comes from the fourth quarter of games already decided, when a trailing quarterback throws it up and a defense picks it off. The turnover still leads. The margin is about a third of what the raw numbers suggest. Simms is closer to right about Sunday than the headline gives him, and the difference is garbage time.

Where the big plays went

Something else the recent numbers say that nobody in the booth is saying: the big play is in mild decline. Defenses have taken the deep ball away, and offenses have answered with efficiency rather than with more shots. The first play of a drive is no more explosive now than it was a decade ago ({{ facts.drive_position_play1_last_era|pct }} against {{ facts.drive_position_play1_first_era|pct }}), and the share of big plays that come in garbage time has sat near {{ facts.garbage_share_mean|pct }} for twenty-seven years. If the modern offense were hunting the chunk play to shorten drives, this is where it would show. It doesn't.

The mechanism isn't there either

The shorter-drive story is the part of the theory that can be checked most directly, and it fails. Drives are longer than they were, not shorter: {{ m0.plays_per_drive|f1 }} plays and about {{ (m0.seconds_per_drive/60)|f1 }} minutes of clock per possession in 1999, {{ m1.plays_per_drive|f1 }} plays and {{ (m1.seconds_per_drive/60)|f1 }} minutes in 2025. The rate at which a defense makes a play - a sack, a takeaway, a tackle behind the line - has fallen from {{ m0.def_play_rate_per_snap|pct }} of snaps to {{ m1.def_play_rate_per_snap|pct }}. There is no rising per-snap danger for an offense to hurry away from; the modern defense gets its hands on fewer plays, not more. Nor does the danger build as a drive goes on. The chance of a defensive play climbs from the first snap to the third - {{ facts.def_play_rate_play1_last_era|pct }} to {{ facts.def_play_rate_play3_last_era|pct }}, which is mostly third down - and then sits flat through the eighth play and beyond ({{ facts.def_play_rate_play8plus_last_era|pct }}). Meanwhile points per drive rose from {{ facts.points_per_drive_first_era|f2 }} to {{ facts.points_per_drive_last_era|f2 }} across the eras while the chance a drive suffers at least one defensive play stayed put. Long drives score. They are not increasingly dangerous. And the teams that generate the most explosive plays don't run shorter drives than everyone else. They run slightly longer ones. Offenses aren't gambling on the big play to escape the defense. They are moving the ball more efficiently across the board, and the big plays come with it.

What has changed dramatically is the thing the theory was supposed to displace. Turnovers have fallen from {{ m0.turnovers_per_game|f1 }} a game to {{ m1.turnovers_per_game|f1 }} - a drop of about forty percent. Quarterbacks throw fewer interceptions, ball carriers lose fewer fumbles, and the rules protect the passer. A turnover is worth as much as it ever was. There are simply fewer of them, which means the turnover battle is a draw more often, and in a draw the big plays decide.

Where Simms is right, for a reason he didn't give

Here is the result that changes the argument. Take every team-season and split it in half. How well does the first half predict the second? Margin of victory - the most information-rich number there is - carries over with a correlation of about {{ p3.margin_per_game.r|f2 }}. Explosive-play differential carries over at {{ p3.explosive_diff_per_game.r|f2 }}. Turnover differential carries over at {{ p3.turnover_diff_per_game.r|f2 }}, barely above nothing, in every era.

Read that as a statement about what kind of thing each number is. A team that is winning the turnover battle in October has, mostly, been lucky, and its luck will not follow it into December. A team that is winning the big-play battle in October is doing something - protecting the quarterback, hitting deep, tackling in space, making the other side drive the length of the field - that it will still be doing in December. Turnovers decide games and then evaporate. Big plays decide games less often and persist. If you want to know who won on Sunday, count the turnovers. If you want to know who is good, count the big plays.

One honest caveat

Part of the persistence gap is arithmetic. A team produces about nine explosive plays a game and gives the ball away barely more than once, and a rare event is measured with more noise than a common one, so some of "turnovers don't repeat" is really "turnovers are rare." Not all of it - the gap is too wide, and it holds year to year as well as half to half - but the site says so where it reports the numbers rather than letting the cleaner story stand alone. There is a second caveat too, stated on the methodology page: explosive plays are yards, yards are points, and so "big plays predict wins" is partly a tautology. Every comparison here is therefore relative - against turnovers, and across time - and the result holds under a definition of explosive that uses expected points instead of yards.

What to take from it

The broadcast version of the theory - that big plays have caught turnovers as the thing that decides a game - is not supported, and the story about shorter drives and defenses waiting to pounce is backwards. But the instinct behind it is sound. The game has fewer turnovers than it used to, so the turnover battle settles fewer games. And the explosive play is the better measure of what a team actually is, because it is the one that shows up again next week. Coaches who build for it aren't chasing a fad. They are chasing the part of winning that they can keep.
