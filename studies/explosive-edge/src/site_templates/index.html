Do big plays decide games now?

{{ facts.n_games|n }} regular-season games, {{ facts.first_season }} to {{ facts.last_season }}, every play of them. The claim under test: explosive plays now belong next to turnovers as the thing that decides who wins.

The short version

  • Turnovers still decide games. In every era since 1999, the turnover differential predicts the winner better than the explosive-play differential does. Knowing only the turnover battle gets you {{ s3.tod_only_accuracy|pct }} of winners right in {{ facts.last_era }}; knowing only the explosive battle gets you {{ s3.epd_only_accuracy|pct }}. A turnover is worth about {{ (-x3.points_per_turnover)|f1 }} points of margin and an explosive play about {{ x3.points_per_explosive|f1 }}, so one turnover equals roughly {{ (-x3.exchange_ratio_turnover_to_explosive)|f1 }} big plays. That exchange rate has barely moved in 27 years.
  • Win the big-play battle and lose the turnover battle, and you usually lose. {{ h1.won_explosive_lost_turnover.win_rate|pct }} of such games in {{ facts.eras[0] }}, {{ h3.won_explosive_lost_turnover.win_rate|pct }} in {{ facts.last_era }}. A little better than it was. Still a losing proposition.
  • But big plays decide seasons. A team's explosive-play differential over the first half of a season predicts its second half with a correlation of {{ p3.explosive_diff_per_game.r|f2 }} ({{ facts.last_era }}). Its turnover differential: {{ p3.turnover_diff_per_game.r|f2 }}. Big plays are a repeatable trait. Turnovers are mostly weather. And explosive differential tracks the main site's True Strength Rating as closely as turnover differential does.
  • The mechanism Simms describes isn't there. Drives have gotten longer, not shorter: {{ m0.plays_per_drive|f1 }} plays and {{ m0.seconds_per_drive|f0 }} seconds per drive in 1999, {{ m1.plays_per_drive|f1 }} plays and {{ m1.seconds_per_drive|f0 }} seconds in 2025. Defenses make a play (sack, takeaway, or tackle for loss) on {{ m1.def_play_rate_per_snap|pct }} of snaps now, down from {{ m0.def_play_rate_per_snap|pct }}. There is no rising per-snap danger for offenses to hurry away from.
  • What did change is that turnovers are vanishing. {{ m0.turnovers_per_game|f1 }} per game in 1999, {{ m1.turnovers_per_game|f1 }} in 2025. The turnover battle is a draw more often than it used to be, and when it is, the explosive battle decides.

Who is good: big plays, because they repeat

{{ tables.persistence_by_era }}

Split every team's season in half and ask how well the first half predicts the second. Margin of victory, the benchmark, holds up at about {{ p3.margin_per_game.r|f2 }}. Explosive-play differential holds up at {{ p3.explosive_diff_per_game.r|f2 }}. Turnover differential barely holds up at all: {{ p3.turnover_diff_per_game.r|f2 }}. That gap is the strongest thing in this study, and it points the way Simms is pointing, for a reason he doesn't quite give: not because big plays swing games more than turnovers do (they don't), but because a team that makes them will keep making them, while a team that is winning the turnover battle in October is mostly getting lucky. Part of that gap is arithmetic - a team has about nine explosive plays a game and barely one turnover, so the rarer event is measured with more noise - and the skill-or-luck page says so. The gap survives the caveat.
