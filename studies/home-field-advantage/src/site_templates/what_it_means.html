What It Means

Fifty-five seasons of final scores, one hypothesis about resale tickets that turned out to be wrong, and a better picture of what home field actually is.

The question we started with

Anyone who has bought a late-December ticket on the secondary market knows the feeling: the home team is 4-10, the upper deck is half visiting colors, and the place sounds like a neutral site. The hypothesis was simple. Season-ticket holders of teams going nowhere sell their seats, visiting fans buy them, and the home edge - already smaller than it used to be - drains out of the building in the last month of the year. If that were true, it would show up two ways: home-field advantage should shrink as the season goes on, and it should shrink most for teams already out of the race.

Neither happens. Not in the 1970s, not in the 2010s, not since. Home field is worth about {{ facts.hfa_early|f1 }} points in the first third of a season and {{ facts.hfa_late|f1 }} in the last third, and that ordering holds in {{ facts.n_decades_late_highest }} of {{ facts.n_decades }} decades with the other a dead heat. The final week alone - the week of resting starters, meaningless games and the emptiest upper decks - is worth {{ facts.final_week|f1 }}. Games involving teams that were already eliminated - by an approximate label built from conference standings, ignoring tiebreakers and division races - carry as much home field as games between contenders. The stadium may look different in December. The scoreboard doesn't notice.

What did change

The real story in the data is the long decline. Through the 1970s, 1980s and 1990s, home field was worth about three points - which, for anyone who has ever looked at a point spread, is exactly the number the betting markets used for decades. Then it started sliding: {{ facts.dec.loc["2000s", "hfa"]|f1 }} in the 2000s, {{ facts.hfa_2010s|f1 }} in the 2010s, {{ facts.hfa_2020s|f1 }} so far in the 2020s. Home teams won six of every ten games in the 1990s. In the 2020s they win barely more than half.

The scores can't tell you why. They can tell you what fits. Charter flights, sleep science, and hotel routines have turned a road trip from an ordeal into an inconvenience. Replay review took away some of what a loud crowd used to extract from officials on close calls. Stadiums got quieter as club seating and corporate sections replaced the bleachers. The rulebook tilted toward offense and away from the kind of game where a crowd could rattle a quarterback into a false start on third-and-short. Every one of those pushes the same direction, and no single one of them can be pulled out of a final score. What can be said is that the number has fallen by more than a point, that the fall is far too large to be noise, and that nothing in the last five seasons suggests it is coming back.

The season with no fans

If the resale hypothesis is wrong, is the crowd irrelevant? The league came close to running that experiment in 2020. For most of the season nobody was in the seats, and home-field advantage that year was {{ facts.hfa_2020|f1 }} points: home teams won half their games. The next season the fans came back and so did the edge, at {{ facts.hfa_2021_25|f1 }} points over 2021-2025.

The first version of this page called that proof. It isn't, and the reason is in this study's own table. Home field in 2019, with every stadium full, was {{ facts.hfa_2019|f1 }} points, a hair below zero, the year before the pandemic. One near-zero season among fifty-six is not remarkable; two in a row, one of them empty, is suggestive and no more. And 2020 was not a clean experiment: there was no preseason, practice was restricted, and attendance limits differed by city and changed through the year, none of it assigned at random. The most that can be said is that the empty season is consistent with the crowd being a large part of home field, and that a real test would compare games under different attendance limits within 2020, allowing for venue and team. That test is not on this site.

What the playoff-race result adds is narrower and firmer: a stadium full of somewhat different people - more visitors, fewer regulars, quieter in a lost season - produces the same home edge as a stadium full of the faithful. Whatever the crowd does, it does not seem to depend on who, exactly, is in the seats.

Why the obvious test gives the wrong answer

There is a reason this question hasn't been settled by someone with a spreadsheet and an afternoon. The obvious test - sort late-season games by whether the home team is still in the race, compare margins to what the teams' ratings predict - produces a striking result: eliminated hosts appear to enjoy nearly {{ facts.naive_elim|f1 }} points of home field, against {{ facts.naive_alive|f1 }} for contenders. Bad teams, it seems, play up at home in December.

They don't. That number is an artifact of how the groups were formed. A team is eliminated because it lost a lot of games early. Its season-long rating includes those losses. Measured against a rating dragged down by the very games that earned it the label, its late games look better than expected almost automatically - and, tellingly, they look better on the road too. Fix it by freezing each team's status before the window you study and rating teams only on games inside that window, so the yardstick knows nothing about the results that defined the groups, and the gap disappears. The playoff race page shows both versions. The wrong one is worth seeing, because it is the one most people would produce.

The Terrible Towel test

The second question was about the fans who travel. Steelers fans are famous for it. So are Packers fans, Cowboys fans, Raiders fans in their day, 49ers fans across the West. If a visiting fan base can turn a road stadium half gold, the home team's edge should shrink when those teams visit - which would show up as a smaller-than-usual gap between how those teams play at home and how they play on the road.

Nothing detectable shows up. The league-average gap is {{ facts.swing_league|f1 }} points. Pittsburgh's is {{ facts.pit_swing|f1 }} - larger than average. The Steelers, for all the towels in the visiting sections, have been a noticeably better team at Three Rivers and Heinz Field than anywhere else, which is the opposite of what traveling fans would produce. San Francisco's gap is {{ facts.sf_swing|f1 }}, a little smaller than average, but no more so than chance would give a random team. Across all {{ facts.n_franchises }} franchises, only {{ facts.n_abs_z_over_2 }} sit more than two standard errors from the league average, which is roughly what you'd expect if every team were exactly the same. {{ facts.swing_max_team }} has the largest gap and {{ facts.swing_min_team }} the smallest; neither is the story anyone tells about traveling fans.

Here too there is a limit to what scores can say. A team whose fans travel well and a team whose home crowd is quiet would look identical in this test: both play about as well away as at home. Separating them would take attendance figures or ticket-market data, and this study doesn't have them. What it can say is that if any fan base is worth points on the road, the effect is smaller than fifty-five seasons of scores can detect: a franchise's swing is measured to about ±{{ facts.swing_se_median|f1 }} points, so a road effect of up to roughly a point per game would still be hiding inside the noise for most teams. Traveling fans are real. They are on television every week. They are not, as far as the scoreboard can tell, worth a field goal, and this study cannot rule out that they are worth a point.

One more distinction. The swing is not the value of a stadium. Denver's {{ facts.den_swing|f1 }}-point swing is how much better the Broncos have played at home than away, pooled across fifty-five seasons and two buildings; it is not an estimate that Mile High adds {{ facts.den_swing|f1 }} points, or any particular number. A venue study would split stadiums and eras, pull small samples toward the league average, allow for travel, rest, altitude and roof, and check whether a venue's edge persists into later seasons before naming a winner. Whether a stadium is worth extra is a question scores can address that way; whether its fans are the reason is one they cannot.

What to take from it

Home field is smaller than it used to be and shows no sign of recovering. It is not eroded by the calendar, by elimination, or by the visiting fans in section 340, as far as scores can tell. It is eroded by everything that has made the NFL a more controlled, more professional, more evenly officiated league, and the one season played without people in the building is consistent with the crowd being most of what is left. That is a cleaner story than the one about resale markets, and it is stated here with the uncertainty it deserves.
