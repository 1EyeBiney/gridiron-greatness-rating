Methodology

One yardstick, applied everywhere, and the three places it needs care.

The data

Every NFL regular-season game from 1970 through 2025: FiveThirtyEight's public game file for 1970-1998 and the nflverse schedule file for 1999 onward, unified into one table by the sibling Gridiron Greatness Rating project, with franchise-stable team codes so a relocated team is one team throughout. Three exclusions: the 1982 strike season (nine games on an irregular schedule and a one-off sixteen-team tournament), the three weeks of 1987 replacement-player games, and neutral-site games, which are kept in the rating fit but carry no home-field advantage and so contribute nothing to any average. Postseason games are not used - the question is about the regular season and its late-season dynamics.

The yardstick: adjusted home margin

For each season, an ordinary-least-squares model fits every game's margin as

home score − away score = rhome − raway + h × (1 if a true home game)

with one rating r per team, constrained to sum to zero, and one shared home-field term h. This is the Massey rating with a home term, fit on a full season at a time. The adjusted home margin of a game is then the actual margin minus the rating difference: how much the home team beat expectation by, before any home-field credit. Averaged over any set of true home games, it estimates the home-field advantage for that set, in points, with a standard error of the mean alongside it. Every table on this site is this average over a different set of games. Home win percentage and raw margin are shown too, but they are not adjusted for team quality and are there for context.

Because the model is refit every season, "expectation" always means how good the two teams were that year, so the 1970s and 2020s are treated identically. Schedules in the pre-1999 seasons have no week numbers in the source data; weeks are assigned from the calendar (Tuesday to Monday), which places a Thursday, Saturday, Sunday and Monday slate in one week and reproduces the true 14-, 16-, 17- and 18-week schedule lengths exactly. Each season is then split into thirds by week number.

Care point one: selecting games on outcomes

A team's playoff situation is a function of its earlier results. Group games by that situation and compare them to a full-season rating and you measure selection, not home field: teams picked for a bad start beat a rating that includes the bad start. The playoff race page shows the artifact in full (it is worth about a point and a half, and it appears at home and on the road alike). The fix used here is to freeze each team's situation entering the window being studied and fit the ratings on that window's games alone. The residuals then contain no information about the results that defined the label, and their group averages are unbiased.

Situation is computed from the standings entering each week, ignoring tiebreakers, with ties counting half a win. "Eliminated" means the team cannot reach the current point total of the last team holding a playoff spot in its conference; "clinched" means no more than that many other teams can still match its total; "3+ games back" is three or more games behind that last spot; everyone else is "in the race". Playoff spots per conference follow the real format: four through 1977, five through 1989, six through 2019, seven since 2020. These are approximate contention categories, not mathematical elimination: the rule works from conference-wide point totals and ignores division titles and tiebreakers, so a team labelled "eliminated" is one that could no longer catch the last wild-card total, and a few teams still alive through a division race or a tiebreaker will be mislabelled. The categories are meant to sort games by how much they mattered to the people in the seats, which is what the resale hypothesis is about.

Care point two: what a rating model can and cannot separate

Suppose eliminated hosts really did have a smaller home edge. The fit has no separate term for that, so it does the next best thing: it lowers those teams' ratings slightly, which makes their home games look a little worse than expected and their road games a little better. Half the effect lands in each role. So the "as host" and "as visitor" averages for any group of teams come out nearly equal whatever the truth is, and only their combination - the home-field advantage across all games involving those teams - is cleanly identified. The site reports that combination as the headline and shows the two roles so the reader can see them agree.

The same logic limits the franchise question. A team whose fans travel would give up less on the road, which is a small home/road swing. But a team with a weak home crowd would also have a small swing. The swing is identified; its cause is not. Separating the two would take attendance or ticket-market data, which this project does not have.

Care point three: what the numbers can't tell you

  • The 2020 season is a natural experiment, not a controlled one. Home field was already near zero in 2019 ({{ pm(facts.hfa_2019, facts.se_2019) }}), the 2020 preseason was cancelled and practice restricted, and attendance limits varied by city and month. The near-zero 2020 figure is consistent with a crowd effect and is reported as suggestive. A within-2020 comparison of games under different attendance limits, allowing for venue and team, has not been done here.
  • A franchise's home/road swing is not a venue estimate, and "no franchise is significantly unusual" means no effect larger than roughly a point per road game is detectable, not that none exists. See the franchise page.
  • The decline across decades is well outside sampling error, but its causes (travel, recovery, replay review, stadium economics, rule changes) are not separable from scores.
  • A single season's home-field advantage has a standard error near 0.8 points. Season-to-season wiggles are mostly noise; the decade averages are the signal.
  • Roof type and division-game flags exist only from 1999.
  • The 1982 exclusion removes one season; nothing else in the analysis changes if it is included with a coarser week assignment, but its final-third fit has too few games for a rating model, so it is left out throughout.

Reproducibility

Everything is scripted. One command re-downloads nothing, re-fits every season, regenerates every table and every page, and the test suite pins every number quoted in the prose to the tables it came from. The repository has the code; the data page has every table as CSV.
