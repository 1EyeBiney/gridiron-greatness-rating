Do teams out of the race lose their home edge?

The resale-market hypothesis, tested the careful way - and a worked example of the trap the careless way falls into.

The hypothesis

Once a team is out of the playoff picture, its season-ticket holders have less reason to go and more reason to sell. Visiting fans buy those seats. The stadium is still full, but it is no longer the home team's crowd, and the home edge should shrink. If that is true, home-field advantage in late-season games hosted by eliminated teams should be measurably smaller than in games hosted by teams still fighting for a spot.

The trap

The obvious test is to label each late-season game by the home team's situation that week and average the adjusted margins. Here is what that produces:

Read naively, eliminated hosts enjoy nearly twice the home edge of contenders - {{ facts.naive_elim|f1 }} points against {{ facts.naive_alive|f1 }}. That is not a home-field effect. It is selection. A team is "eliminated" because it lost a lot of games earlier in the season, and its full-season rating averages those losses in. Compared to that rating, its late-season games look good almost by construction: the bad games that defined the label are the same games dragging down the yardstick it's measured against. The effect shows up for eliminated teams on the road, too, which is how you know it isn't about the stadium. Any analysis that groups games by a team's record and compares them to a rating built from that record has this problem.

The fix

Two changes remove the contamination. First, each team's situation is frozen entering the window being studied - the final three weeks, or the final third of the season - so the label depends only on games before the window. Second, the ratings used to set expectation are fit on the window's games alone, so they contain no information about the earlier results that produced the label. The residuals are then independent of the label, and their average over any group of games is an unbiased estimate of that group's home-field advantage.

One thing scores cannot do, even then. A rating model spreads any team-specific home effect across both of that team's roles: if eliminated hosts really had a smaller edge, the fit would lower those teams' ratings a little and the shortfall would appear half in their home games and half in their road games. The "as host" and "as visitor" columns below are therefore nearly identical by construction and should not be read separately. The cleanly identified quantity is the home-field advantage across all games involving teams in a given situation. If eliminated hosts were giving up even a point of home edge, that number would be visibly lower for eliminated teams than for contenders. That is the test.

What it means

The fans in the seats may well change late in the year for teams going nowhere. If they do, it doesn't reach the scoreboard in any amount this much data can detect - and this is a lot of data. Set against the 2020 result on the venues page, where removing the crowd entirely removed the home edge, the picture is consistent: a stadium full of people helps the home team, and a stadium full of somewhat different people helps it about the same amount.
