Does home field still matter?

{{ facts.n_games|n }} regular-season games, {{ facts.first_season }} to {{ facts.last_season }}, one question: how many points is playing at home worth, and does that number fade as the season goes on?

The short version

  • Home field is worth about {{ facts.hfa_all|f1 }} points over the whole period, after accounting for how good the two teams were. It has been shrinking: roughly {{ facts.hfa_1990s|f1 }} points in the 1990s, {{ facts.hfa_2010s|f1 }} in the 2010s, {{ facts.hfa_2020s|f1 }} so far in the 2020s. Home teams won {{ facts.winpct_1990s|pct }} of games in the 1990s and {{ facts.winpct_2020s|pct }} in the 2020s.
  • It does not fade late in the season. It grows. Home field is worth {{ facts.hfa_early|f1 }} points in the first third of the season, {{ facts.hfa_mid|f1 }} in the middle, and {{ facts.hfa_late|f1 }} in the final third. The final third is the highest of the three in {{ facts.n_decades_late_highest }} of the {{ facts.n_decades }} decades since 1970{% if facts.decades_late_not_highest %}, and in the {{ facts.decades_late_not_highest|join(" and ") }} it trails the best third by {{ facts.late_shortfall_max|f2 }} points, a tie{% endif %}. The final week of the season alone comes in at {{ facts.final_week|f1 }}.
  • Teams out of the playoff race do not lose their home edge. In the final three weeks, games involving a team that had already been eliminated carry a home-field advantage of {{ facts.elim_last3|f1 }} points; games involving a team still in the race carry {{ facts.alive_last3|f1 }}. That gap is well inside the noise. Sorted by games behind the last playoff spot, the pattern is flat.
  • Crowds are the mechanism, though. The 2020 season, played in mostly empty stadiums, is the one season in the sample with essentially no home-field advantage: {{ facts.hfa_2020|f1 }} points, against {{ facts.hfa_2015_19|f1 }} in the five seasons before and {{ facts.hfa_2021_25|f1 }} in the five after.
  • No franchise's fans travel well enough to show up in the scores. Every team's home/road swing sits within about two standard errors of the league average. The Steelers, the fan base most often credited with taking over road stadiums, have one of the larger swings, not one of the smaller ones.

How much is home field worth, and where did it go?

{{ tables.by_decade }}

Every number on this site is the same thing measured on a different group of games: how much the home team beat expectation by, where expectation is the difference in the two teams' season-long strength ratings. Averaged over a group of games, that is the home-field advantage for that group, in points. The methodology page has the details; the important part is that it's the same yardstick in every table, so they can be compared.

The decline is the clearest result here. The 1970s, 1980s, and 1990s all sit near {{ facts.hfa_1970s|f1 }} points. The 2000s dip, the 2010s dip further, and the 2020s are at {{ facts.hfa_2020s|f1 }}. The difference between the 1990s and the 2020s is more than three standard errors, so it isn't sampling noise. The usual explanations - easier travel, better sleep and recovery routines on the road, replay review removing some of what the crowd used to get from officials, and stadiums with more corporate and resale seating - all point the same direction and none can be separated from the others with scores alone. The season-by-season table shows how much year-to-year noise sits on top of the trend: a single season's home-field advantage has a standard error near 0.8 points, so the lowest and highest seasons ({{ facts.season_min }} at {{ facts.season_min_hfa|f1 }}, {{ facts.season_max }} at {{ facts.season_max_hfa|f1 }}) are mostly noise around the decade averages.

Does it fade as the season goes on?

{{ tables.by_phase }}

This was the hypothesis the study started with: once teams fall out of the race, their season-ticket holders sell on the resale market, visiting fans fill the seats, and the home edge thins. The data say the opposite about the league as a whole. The final third of the season is the strongest part for home teams by about {{ facts.late_minus_early|f1 }} points over the first third. It is the strongest third in {{ facts.n_decades_late_highest }} of {{ facts.n_decades }} decades and tied in the other, and the 2020s follow the pattern ({{ facts.early_2020s|f1 }} early, {{ facts.late_2020s|f1 }} late). Across the {{ facts.n_seasons }} seasons, {{ facts.n_seasons_late_gt_early }} had a bigger home-field advantage in the final third than in the first.

Weather is the obvious candidate for a late-season bump, and it isn't the answer, or at least not the whole one: indoor home fields show the same late rise as outdoor ones ({{ facts.indoor_late|f1 }} indoors, {{ facts.outdoor_late|f1 }} outdoors in the final third, against {{ facts.indoor_early|f1 }} and {{ facts.outdoor_early|f1 }} early). The venues page has those tables. Week 1 is one of the two weakest weeks for home teams in the 1999-2025 data ({{ facts.week1|f1 }} points, with week {{ facts.weakest_weeks|reject("equalto", 1)|first }} the other), which fits a simpler story: early in the year the teams are the least settled and the least known, and home field is a smaller share of what decides a game.

Do eliminated teams lose their home edge?

{{ tables.last3 }}

This is the resale-market hypothesis tested directly. It needs care, because a team's playoff status is defined by its own earlier results, and grouping games by status then comparing them to a full-season rating produces a mirage - eliminated teams "beat expectation" by {{ facts.naive_elim|f1 }} points at home under that naive method, simply because they were selected for a bad start. The playoff race page walks through the artifact and the fix: freeze each team's status entering a late-season window, and rate teams only on the games inside that window.

Done that way, the answer is no. In the final three weeks, home-field advantage in games involving an eliminated team is {{ pm(facts.elim_last3, facts.elim_last3_se) }} points; in games involving a team still in the race, {{ pm(facts.alive_last3, facts.alive_last3_se) }}. Teams three or more games out of a spot: {{ facts.gb3_last3|f1 }}. Teams holding a spot: {{ facts.spot_last3|f1 }}. There is one honest limit, explained on that page: from scores alone you cannot separate an eliminated team's lost edge at home from any edge its opponents gain when hosting it, so the cleanly measured quantity is the home-field advantage across all games involving such teams. If half-empty stadiums were costing eliminated hosts even a point, that number would move. It doesn't.

Then what does the crowd do?

{{ tables.covid }}

Plenty, apparently. The 2020 season is the closest thing to a controlled experiment the league has ever run: same teams, same travel, same officials, nearly no fans. Home-field advantage fell to {{ pm(facts.hfa_2020, facts.se_2020) }} points and home teams won half their games. It came back the next year. Fans in the building are a real part of home field. What the playoff-race result says is that the mix of fans in a nearly full stadium late in the year doesn't measurably change it - which is different from having no fans at all.

Do some fan bases travel well enough to matter?

The question was whether teams like the Steelers or 49ers, whose fans famously show up in road stadiums, blunt the home team's edge. The measurable version is each franchise's home/road swing: how much better it plays at home than on the road, both measured against expectation. A team whose fans neutralise road crowds should have a small swing. The league-average swing is {{ facts.swing_league|f1 }} points (twice the home-field advantage). The franchise table runs from {{ facts.swing_min_team }} at {{ facts.swing_min|f1 }} to {{ facts.swing_max_team }} at {{ facts.swing_max|f1 }}, and only {{ facts.n_abs_z_over_2 }} of {{ facts.n_franchises }} franchises sit two or more standard errors from the league average - about what chance alone would produce. Pittsburgh's swing is {{ facts.pit_swing|f1 }} (z = {{ facts.pit_z|f2 }}), larger than average. San Francisco's is {{ facts.sf_swing|f1 }} (z = {{ facts.sf_z|f2 }}), a little smaller than average but nowhere near significant. Dallas (z = {{ facts.dal_z|f2 }}), Green Bay (z = {{ facts.gb_z|f2 }}), and the Raiders (z = {{ facts.oak_z|f2 }}) are unremarkable. Traveling fans are real and visible on television. Their effect on the scoreboard, if there is one, is smaller than fifty-five seasons of scores can detect.
