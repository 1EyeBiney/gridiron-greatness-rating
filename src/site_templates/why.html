Why This Exists

A hunch, a favorite team, and a question about how you'd even compare a 1985 team to a 2025 one.

It started with a hunch

Going into the most recent Super Bowl, I had a feeling New England was a weaker opponent than a 14-3 record made them look - not a bad team, but not the kind of team that usually gets to that game. I had them somewhere around eighth-best in the league in my head. I wanted to know if the numbers agreed, and I didn't want a rating that just counted wins. I wanted one that knew who you beat, by how much, and how good those teams actually were.

The model here rated that Patriots team fifth in the league that season - three spots higher than I had them, so the numbers liked New England a little more than I did. But the larger point held. Of the 112 teams that have played in a Super Bowl since 1970, about three-quarters were rated stronger going in than the 2025 Patriots were. Seattle, rated the best team in football that year and one of the top twenty team-seasons of the entire post-merger era, won 29-13. The gap between the two teams was one of the fifteen largest in Super Bowl history. That's the first thing this project told me, and it told me before I'd built anything to look at other than a table.

Then I wanted to know about my teams

I grew up a Bears fan, which means I'm an 1985 Bears fan, and I wanted to see how they'd hold up against every other great team since the merger without anybody's memory doing the grading. They come out second of all 1,669 team-seasons, behind only the 1996 Packers - a defense rated two-and-a-third standard deviations above that year's league, one loss all season, a 46-10 Super Bowl. I'll take it.

Then Peyton Manning arrived in Indiana, my home state, and I had a second team before I'd finished deciding whether I wanted one. The Manning Colts surprised me here. Their best team by this measure wasn't the one that won the Super Bowl - the 2006 champions rate as only the fifth-best Colts team of that run. The peak was 2004, a team that lost in the divisional round in New England and never got the chance to prove it. That's a pattern you'll see a lot on this site: the trophy and the strength are two different things, and the site keeps them as two different numbers on purpose.

The 2002 Buccaneers are on my list for a less defensible reason: Mike Alstott. The A-Train came out of Purdue, and Purdue ties run deep where I'm from, so I'd been watching him bull through tacklers for years before he scored in the Super Bowl. It turns out that team holds up on its own merits, too - thirteenth all-time, the best defense of any champion since the merger, an offense that rated dead on league average. Alstott was the exception on that side of the ball, and I'd have told you so at the time. The What It All Means page has a section on them.

And the Patriots through the whole run: nine of the top hundred team-seasons since 1970 are Patriots teams, more than any franchise except the 49ers. The 2007 team is eighth all-time and didn't win. The 2004 team is fourteenth and did. If you want the argument about which one was better, the numbers are on the Super Bowl pages.

What comparing across eras actually means here

Every rating on this site is measured against that team's own season - how far above or below the league that year. So a 2.8 in 1985 and a 2.3 in 2025 are the same kind of number: standard deviations above average. That's what makes a 1985 team and a 2025 team comparable at all.

It's worth being honest about what that does and doesn't tell you. It won't tell you who'd win if the '85 Bears lined up against the 2025 Seahawks - different rules, different athletes, different everything. What it tells you is how dominant each team was within its own time, and lets you put those dominances side by side. That's the argument worth having anyway. Nobody at the bar is actually proposing a time machine.

The one that made me look twice

I went looking for the 1990s Cowboys and couldn't find them near the top. Three titles in four years and not one of those teams cracks the top fifteen. Then I looked closer: 1992, 1993, 1994, and 1995 rate eighteenth, twenty-fourth, twenty-third, and twenty-seventh all-time - four straight seasons in almost exactly the same place, including the one year (1994) they didn't win it all. That's not a team that got lucky once or twice. That's a team that was the same very good team four years running, and the two years it lost, it lost to the 49ers teams that rate third and nineteenth all-time. The dynasty is real; it just didn't have one towering peak. The data changed how I'd describe it, and that's the point.

There are more of those in here - the perfect 1972 Dolphins rating below a hundred other teams, two Giants champions in the bottom three of all Super Bowl winners, a 15-1 Vikings team that never got to the game. The What It All Means page walks through the big ones. The rest are in the tables, and in the rankings, for anyone who wants to go looking.
