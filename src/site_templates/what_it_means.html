What It All Means

Fifty-six seasons, 1,669 teams, one number for how good each of them really was - and what that number says about the teams people still argue about.

Every team on this site gets a True Strength Rating: how far above or below its own league it played that year, measured in standard deviations, adjusted for who it played and by how much it won. A 2.0 means a team was two standard deviations better than average - roughly, the best team in the league in a normal year. Anything above 2.5 has happened eight times since the 1970 merger. Below is what the list looks like from the top, with the history to go with the numbers. Records and scores are the real ones. The rankings are this project's, and they rank dominance relative to each team's own league; who would win a cross-era matchup is a different question, and no score-based model answers it.

The top of the mountain

1996 Green Bay Packers (2.89, first all-time). Brett Favre's second straight MVP season, Reggie White anchoring a defense that rates 1.85 above the league, and an offense at 2.47 - the only team in the top ten rated that far above average on both sides of the ball at once. They went 13-3, beat New England 35-21 in Super Bowl XXXI, and by this measure were the most complete team of the post-merger era. Not the most famous. The most complete.

1985 Chicago Bears (2.79, second). The 46 defense. One loss, on a Monday night in Miami, and a 46-10 Super Bowl that was over before halftime. Their defense rates 2.33 above the league - among champions, only the 2002 Buccaneers' rates higher - and it is the reason a 15-1 team with a good-not-great offense rates second in fifty-six years. If you grew up believing this was the best defense you'd ever see, the numbers are not going to argue with you very hard.

1994 San Francisco 49ers (2.74, third). Steve Young's MVP year and a 49-26 Super Bowl in which he threw six touchdowns. This is the highest-rated offense of any Super Bowl champion - 3.13 standard deviations above the league, a number only three other teams have ever reached, and none of those three won the title. Their defense was ordinary. It didn't matter.

1978 Pittsburgh Steelers (2.58, fourth) and 1978 Dallas Cowboys (2.52, sixth). The two best teams of the 1970s played each other in Super Bowl XIII, Pittsburgh winning 35-31 - the only Super Bowl in which both participants rate in the top six of all time. If you want to know why people who watched that game still talk about it, that's why.

1989 San Francisco 49ers (2.57, fifth). Joe Montana's best statistical season and a 55-10 Super Bowl over Denver, still the most lopsided ever. Between the 1989 and 1994 teams, San Francisco owns two of the top five team-seasons since the merger - and, with twelve top-hundred seasons, the deepest run of sustained excellence of any franchise in the database.

The best teams that didn't win

This is where the site earns its two-number design. True Strength measures how good a team was. Accomplishment measures what it won. They agree most of the time. The disagreements are the stories.

2007 New England Patriots (2.51, eighth). Sixteen and oh, an offense rated 3.08 above the league - one of only four offenses in fifty-six years to clear 3.0 - and then the Giants, 17-14. By this project's measure the Giants were the eighth-best team in the league that year and the Patriots the best; it is the largest upset in Super Bowl history by rating gap, and it isn't close. The undefeated season is real. So is the loss. The ratings hold both.

2001 St. Louis Rams (2.51, seventh). The second year of the Greatest Show on Turf, an offense rated 3.15 above the league - higher than the 1994 49ers, second in the entire database only to Peyton Manning's 2013 Broncos - and a 20-17 loss to a Patriots team rated sixth in the league that year. Second-largest upset in Super Bowl history. The Patriots dynasty begins with the second-biggest surprise the game has produced.

1983 Washington (2.37, eleventh). Fourteen and two, the defending champions, an offense at 2.69 - and a 38-9 loss to the Raiders in Super Bowl XVIII that nobody who watched the regular season saw coming. One of the great single-game collapses by a great team.

1998 Minnesota Vikings (2.16, twenty-first). Fifteen and one. Randy Moss as a rookie, Randall Cunningham reborn, an offense rated 2.66 above the league. They lost the NFC Championship Game at home, in overtime, to a Falcons team rated well below them - and never reached the Super Bowl. The second-highest-rated team since the merger not to play in one.

1995 San Francisco 49ers (2.21, nineteenth). The highest-rated team since 1970 that didn't even make the Super Bowl. Defending champions, 11-5, rated the best team in the league that year with an offense at 1.98 and a defense at 2.19 - and out in the divisional round, at home, 27-17 to a Green Bay team rated third. That is a better team than a dozen Super Bowl winners on this site, and its January lasted one afternoon. Sometimes the best team in football goes home early. The model doesn't pretend otherwise.

2023 Baltimore Ravens (2.14, twenty-second). The best team of the 2020s that didn't reach the Super Bowl, and it isn't close - no other team this decade that missed the game rates above 1.9. Lamar Jackson's second MVP season, 13-4, the top seed in the AFC, an offense at 1.60 and a defense at 1.83, balance nobody else in the league had that year - and then a 17-10 loss in the AFC Championship Game, at home, to a Kansas City team rated fifth. Kansas City went on to win the title. Baltimore went into the offseason as the best team in football by this measure, with nothing to show for it but the number.

The dynasty that didn't have a peak

Three titles in four years should put the 1990s Cowboys near the top of any list, and they aren't - not one of those teams cracks the top fifteen. Look closer and you'll see why. The 1992, 1993, 1994, and 1995 Cowboys rate eighteenth, twenty-fourth, twenty-third, and twenty-seventh all-time: four straight seasons in nearly the same place, including 1994, the year they didn't win. That was the same team; it just ran into the third-best team in fifty-six years. Dallas didn't have one towering season. It had four very good ones in a row, which is its own kind of rare - only three franchises have seven or more seasons in the all-time top hundred, and Dallas is one of them, alongside San Francisco and New England.

The perfect season that isn't at the top

The 1972 Miami Dolphins went 17-0, the only perfect season in NFL history, and rate ninety-ninth all-time - a 1.58, good, not great. That is the sharpest example of the difference between the two numbers on this site. Their Accomplishment score is 30, the maximum, the only team ever to hit it: undefeated, division title, best record, three playoff wins, the trophy. But True Strength asks a different question - how much better were you than the teams you actually played - and this is almost entirely a schedule story. Miami won big, by more than fifteen points a game, but the opponents they beat rate as the third-softest schedule any champion has faced in fifty-six years, and the model discounts the margins accordingly. Their 1973 team, which lost twice against a tougher slate, rates higher (1.92, fortieth). Perfect record. Very good team. Those are both true, and the site says both.

The champion with no offense

The 2002 Tampa Bay Buccaneers (2.35, thirteenth all-time) are the purest defensive champion in the database. Their offense rates at -0.01 - dead on league average, one of only three Super Bowl winners in fifty-six years to finish the season with a below-average offense. Their defense rates 2.75 above the league, the best of any champion, ahead of the 1985 Bears. Jon Gruden's first season in Tampa, Monte Kiffin's defense with Derrick Brooks, Warren Sapp, John Lynch, and Ronde Barber, a 12-4 record, and a 48-21 Super Bowl over the Raiders team Gruden had coached the year before - a game in which that defense scored three touchdowns by itself. They were the best team in football that year by this measure, and they got there with an offense that was never anything more than adequate. If the 1994 49ers are the proof that an offense can carry a team to the title, this is the proof for the other side of the ball.

The champions the numbers didn't believe in

Eight Super Bowl winners rate below 1.25 - roughly "a top-eight team," not "the best team in football." Two of them are the New York Giants: the 2007 team (rated eighth in the league) that beat the undefeated Patriots, and the 2011 team (rated ninth, 9-7 in the regular season) that beat the Patriots again, 21-17. Same franchise, same opponent, four years apart, both times the lowest-rated champion of their decade. Whatever that organization figured out about January, it figured it out twice.

The lowest-rated champion of all is the 1970 Baltimore Colts (0.64, seventh in the league that year), who won Super Bowl V over Dallas 16-13 in a game remembered mostly for its turnovers. The 2012 Ravens, 2001 Patriots, 2015 Broncos, 2021 Rams, and 1980 Raiders round out the list. None of them were bad teams. None of them were the best team in football that year, either, and all of them have the trophy anyway - which is the whole argument for keeping Accomplishment as its own number instead of folding it into the rating.

What the eras say

Take each decade's ten best team-seasons and average them, and the 1990s come out on top (2.36) - the Cowboys, 49ers, Packers, and Broncos all producing top-fifteen teams in the same ten years. The 1980s and 1970s follow closely. The 2020s, so far, are the lowest (1.92). Read that carefully: it doesn't mean football got worse. It means the best team in a given year now stands a little less far above the pack than it used to - which is roughly what you'd expect from thirty-two teams, a hard salary cap, and a draft designed to level things out. The gap between first and average has narrowed. That's parity working.

Franchises, not seasons

Average every team's rating across all fifty-six seasons and the Steelers lead the franchises that have existed the whole time (0.56 above the league, on average, for half a century), with Dallas (0.51), San Francisco (0.41), and New England (0.40) behind them. The Ravens, with only thirty seasons, edge them all at 0.62. At the other end, the Cardinals (-0.54), Browns (-0.47), Buccaneers (-0.44), and Jets (-0.39) have spent most of the post-merger era below average. Fans of those teams did not need a model to tell them that. Now they have one.

A word about the purple and gold

This site is dressed in Vikings colors, so it's fair to say where Minnesota lands. Their best team since the merger is that 1998 group (2.16, twenty-first all-time), and the 1970 team (1.98, thirty-fourth) is not far behind. Both were the best team in the league that year by this measure. Neither played in the Super Bowl. Minnesota has produced ten seasons rated 1.1 or better and reached four Super Bowls - three of them in this dataset, in 1973, 1974, and 1976, all losses - and in each of those three years it was rated the fourth-best team in the league, never the best. The two seasons it was the best team in football, it didn't get there. If you were looking for the numbers behind a particular kind of heartbreak, they're on the 1998 season page.

How to use the rest of this site

Every claim above comes from the tables here, and every table is one click away. Pick a season to see all thirty-two teams ranked. Pick a Super Bowl to see both teams side by side. The rankings collect the arguments - greatest champions, biggest upsets, records that flattered their teams - and the data downloads have everything, in case you'd rather run the numbers yourself. The methodology page explains how the ratings are built, in plain language.
