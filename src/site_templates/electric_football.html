The Story of Electric Football

Why this site looks the way it does.

In 1947, a man named Norman Sas took over his family's toy company and turned a novelty vibrating-motor gadget into Electric Football: a metal game board painted like a gridiron, wired to a small motor that buzzed the whole field the instant you flipped the switch. Little plastic players, each standing on a weighted round base with a tiny adjustable dial, were lined up in a formation. You clipped a scrap of foam shaped like a football onto your ball carrier, hit the switch, and the entire board shuddered to life.

What happened next was never quite what you drew up. The vibration sent every player buzzing across the tin in whatever direction its dial and its base's felt feet happened to favor that day - in a straight line if you were lucky, in a slow circle if you weren't, and directly into a pileup with three other players more often than either team's coach would have liked. Getting a player to run an actual sweep was closer to a physics experiment than a play call. That unpredictability was the whole game: half the fun, and on a bad night, all of the fight with your brother over whether that "block" counted.

It sold for decades anyway - officially licensed with NFL team paint jobs from the 1960s on, a fixture of Christmas mornings for generations of kids who grew up wanting to command a team of their own. A small but very dedicated community of adult hobbyists still customizes, repaints, and competes with electric football figures today, decades after the rest of the toy aisle moved on to video games.

This project's visual theme is a small tribute to all of that: two old friends who both spent time hunched over a buzzing metal board as kids, watching purple-and-gold figures try (and sometimes fail) to do what they were told. The illustrations scattered around this site were generated with AI, styled after that specific memory - not photos of any real game, and not using any real NFL team's actual logos, just the colors and the spirit of it.
