How It Was Made

One person with the questions and a lifetime of watching football. One AI with the tools. A few days of work. This page is about the way that went, because it is a new way to build something like this and the method mattered as much as the math.

The division of labor

Brian brought the questions - every one of the studies here started as something he had wondered about for years, or heard argued on a broadcast, and wanted settled. He also brought the judgment calls: which teams to feature, what the site should look like, when a result felt wrong and needed a second look, what he was and wasn't willing to claim. Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, did the building: finding and checking the data, writing the models and the code, running the analyses, drafting the pages, and testing all of it. Neither role would have produced this alone. The questions without the tools would have stayed bar arguments. The tools without the questions would have produced tables nobody asked for.

The working rules

A handful of rules were set at the start and held throughout. They are why the numbers on these pages can be trusted, and they are the part most worth copying.

  • Plan first, in writing. Each study began as a plan document - the question stated precisely, the data source and its license, the definitions fixed before any analysis ran, the analyses listed, the checkpoints named. The plans are in the repository and were updated as decisions were made, so a reader can see not only what was done but why.
  • Every number is reproducible. Nothing is typed in by hand. One command rebuilds every table and every page from the raw game files. Raw data is never edited; every transformation is a script. The finished site is not even stored - it is regenerated on every publish.
  • The prose is tested like the code. A sentence like "the final third of the season is the strongest for home teams in every decade" is a claim about data, and it can go stale or simply be wrong. So every such claim is pinned to the tables by an automated check, and the numbers quoted in the text are inserted from the tables at build time. Those checks caught more than half a dozen confident-sounding errors in first drafts before any of them was published: a "best offense" that was actually second, a "highest in every decade" that was highest in five of six, a franchise named as smallest that was second-smallest.
  • Review before believing. When results arrived, they were reviewed for the ways an analysis can fool its author. The home-field study's first pass showed eliminated teams gaining home field late in the year - a striking result that turned out to be an artifact of grouping games by a team's own record. The fix, and the wrong version, are both on that site so the next person doesn't fall into the same hole.
  • Ask before acting. Publishing, fetching a new data source, changing a repository setting - anything outward-facing or hard to reverse - waited for Brian's explicit go-ahead. Sources were checked for license terms before a byte was downloaded, and one candidate dataset was skipped because its terms didn't allow it.
  • Say what the data can't say. Each study has a page that names its limits. Scores can't separate a traveling fan base from a quiet home crowd. Big plays are yards and yards are points. A rating built across eras says how dominant a team was in its own time, not who would win a time-machine game. Those caveats are on the pages, not in a footnote.

How the work actually flowed

The first study, the rating itself, was built in six planned phases over a couple of days: data, models, era normalization, validation, the descriptive scores, and the site. Between phases, Brian switched to a second AI model to review the first one's work - a deliberate fresh pair of eyes - and several improvements came out of those reviews, including a fix to how one model was tuned that had quietly used data it shouldn't have seen.

The later studies were built faster, and differently. By then the pattern was established, so the plan document for each new study was written to be handed to smaller, more specialized AI agents: one ingested the play-by-play data and built the per-game aggregate, two ran the analyses in parallel, one built the site generator. The coordinating model reviewed each hand-off. That review caught two real bugs in the big-play study before any analysis ran on the data - fumbles on punts charged to the wrong team, and a quirk in how the source labels franchises that had left nearly a thousand team-games unmatched. Then it wrote the prose, and the claim checks caught its own overstatements in turn.

The illustrations came from a third tool. The photorealistic figurines on every page were generated in an image model, all as follow-up prompts in a single conversation so the style stayed consistent, with opposing teams shown as bear heads, lion heads, cheeseheads, cowboy hats, jaguars, horses, and a colonial hat rather than any real team's trademark. Brian chose the casts.

What was harder than expected

Not the models. The models are standard, and they are described in plain language on each methodology page. The hard parts were the ones that would trip up any careful analyst: an early week of the season that isn't a week, a franchise code that changes when a team moves, a strike season that breaks every assumption about a schedule, a statistic that looks like a discovery and is really a selection effect. Every one of those is recorded in the decision logs in the repository, with the date and what was done about it.

What was easier than expected

Going from a question to a first honest answer. The home-field study went from "I wonder whether this is true" to a complete, tested, published site in an afternoon. The big-play study, with a new data source of over a million plays, took about the same. That speed is the genuinely new thing here, and it is why the working rules above matter so much: when answers come that fast, the discipline that keeps them honest has to be built into the process, because there is no time for it to be an afterthought.

Everything is open

The code, the plans, the decision logs, the tests, and every table are in the public repository. The game data comes from FiveThirtyEight (1970-1998, MIT license) and nflverse (1999-present, CC BY 4.0), with thanks to both. If you find a number you think is wrong, the repository is the place to say so - and the chances are good that a test will be written for it.
